# What he asked for after reviewing drafts

He reviewed a 54-item grade 3 pool item by item (November 2026). These are his standing requests. They apply to every item and set unless `ayarlar.md` says otherwise. Each rule names the reason, so apply its spirit to cases not listed.

## A Turkish lesson, not a science lesson

- **Texts are Turkish-lesson texts.** Prefer a story, tale, fable, anecdote (fıkra), poem, dialogue, diary entry or message, with a character, a small event and something felt. A short informative text is fine when the answer comes from reading it (for example finding its topic), not from knowing the subject.
- **No item whose key is a school-subject fact.** He rejected items whose answer was a science fact: ice melts when it warms, a magnet attracts iron, hens lay eggs. If the item needs prior knowledge (as in "ön bilgileri ile bağlantı kurar"), call on everyday and social knowledge: manners, customs, feelings, family and school life, well-known sayings.
- **Literary sources.** When a text is wanted from literature, retell public-domain folk material in your own words for the grade: Nasreddin Hoca anecdotes, Keloğlan and other Anatolian tales, Aesop and La Fontaine fables. Use passages from modern children's books only when he has put the text into his work folder, keep them short, and name the author and the work under the text, as publishers do. Never quote a copyrighted book from memory.

## Topics

- **Never use:** planting seeds or saplings, growing plants, watering a pot, a seed sprouting. He sees these in almost every booklet and asked that they not appear at all.
- **Recycling:** at most one item in a whole set.
- **No hunting or fishing,** even by animals in a tale (a bear catching fish counts).
- **Vary the settings** across a set or pool: not three birthdays, not three snowy days.

## Natural writing

He said many items were "obviously AI-written". Signs he means:
- every text has the same three short sentences and a stated moral
- a child's name in every sentence, flat events with no turn
- options that are one-word swaps of each other

Write the way a children's author would: concrete details, varied sentence openings, a small turn or surprise, natural dialogue with the speech dash (—).

**Plots must make sense.** Every action needs a believable reason. He rejected an item where a child turned on a hose to water the garden and then walked off to play, an event invented only to give the item a rule-breaker.

## Distractors that tempt

His most frequent note was "çeldiricilerde kalite artmalı": the wrong options were too easy to rule out. A good distractor:
- uses words, people or details that are really in the text or picture
- is partly true, or true but not what the stem asks (a detail instead of the topic, an event from the wrong point in time)
- fits the title alone or the picture alone, but not both together (prediction items)

A distractor about something absent from the stimulus (a shop when the picture shows children playing) is too simple. The one-key rule still holds: tempting is not defensible.

**Picture-prediction items** must not be solvable at a glance. Give the picture a detail the student must combine with the title.

## Spelling, capital letters and punctuation

Give the rule first. The stimulus states one or two rules as prior information (a short rule box), and the stem asks the student to apply it, for example "Aşağıdakilerden hangisinde bu kurala uymayan bir kullanım vardır?". A bare "which sentence is correct" with no rule is the weaker form.

## Correspondence

Children write to each other with digital messages today. Build correspondence items on a phone or tablet message, a class or family message group, or an e-mail, not a paper letter or postcard. Show the message as a picture placeholder of a message screen with the text written out.

## Audience

For "hedef kitle" items, write for primary-school readers (classmates, younger pupils at the school), not kindergarten children.
